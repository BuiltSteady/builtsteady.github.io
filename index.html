The program

Beginner full body, 3 days a week

Train on three non-back-to-back days, like Monday, Wednesday, Friday. Each day hits your whole body. Pick the intensity that matches where you are right now. Same exercises, different sets, reps, rest, and effort. The program follows the American College of Sports Medicine's guidelines: every major muscle trained at least twice a week. See sources.

Not sure which level?

Take the 5-question quiz and get a suggestion.
1. How long have you been lifting consistently?
2. Can you do the squat, bench, and deadlift with good form?
3. How do you usually feel the day after a workout?
4. Do you have any current injury or joint pain?
5. Do you train with a spotter or safety arms?
  • Progressive overloadWhen you hit the top of the rep range on every set with good form, add a little weight next time (usually 5 lb on lower body, 2.5–5 lb on upper body).
  • Warm up every timeDo the 5-minute warm-up, then 2–3 lighter sets of your first lift before your working sets.
  • Move up one level at a timeStart at Light or Medium. Only move up when the current level feels routine and your form stays the same on the last rep.
  • Write it downLog every set in a notebook or your phone. If you don't track it, you can't tell if you're getting stronger.

Warm-up

5-minute warm-up

Do this before Day A, B, or C. It raises your body temperature and gets your joints ready, so your first heavy set doesn't feel like a shock. Press Start to be guided through it.

  1. Light cardio3 minBike, brisk walk, or easy jog. You should still be able to talk.
  2. Arm circles10 each waySmall circles growing bigger, forward then backward.
  3. Bodyweight squats10 repsSlow and controlled, as deep as feels comfortable.
  4. Hip hinges10 repsHands on hips, push your hips back with a flat back, then stand.
  5. Glute bridges10 repsLie on your back, knees bent, squeeze your glutes to lift your hips.
  6. Wall slides10 repsBack and arms against a wall, slide your arms up and down slowly.
  7. Ramp-up sets3 setsOn your first lift: light weight × 10, about half your working weight × 5, about three-quarters × 3.

        Nutrition basics

        You can't out-lift an empty plate

        These are general guidelines commonly used for healthy adults who lift. Everyone's needs are different. For a personal plan, especially if you have a health condition or allergy, talk to a doctor or registered dietitian.

        Protein

        Muscle is built from protein. Many lifters aim for roughly 0.7–1 gram per pound of bodyweight a day, spread across 3–4 meals. Get it from food first.

        Calories

        To gain muscle you usually need to eat a little more than you burn. A small, steady surplus with slow weight gain over time beats eating everything in sight.

        Sleep

        Teens need about 8–10 hours a night and adults at least 7. Poor sleep means slower recovery, weaker workouts, and less muscle gained.

        ProteinChicken, eggs, Greek yogurt, fish, lean beef, beans, milk
        CarbsRice, oats, potatoes, pasta, bread, fruit. Fuel for training.
        FatsPeanut butter, nuts, olive oil, avocado, whole eggs
        ProduceFruits and vegetables at most meals for vitamins and fiber

        Body basics

        How muscle actually grows

        The short version of what kinesiology and exercise science say about building muscle, which is called hypertrophy.

        1. TensionLifting challenging weights puts tension on muscle fibers. That's the main signal telling your body to grow.
        2. SignalThat stress tells your body to repair the fibers and make them a bit bigger and stronger.
        3. RecoveryRepair happens over the next day or two using protein, calories, and sleep. That's why rest days matter.
        4. Repeat & addYour body adapts, so you add a little weight or reps over time. That's progressive overload.
        Rep
        One full movement of an exercise, down and up.
        Set
        A group of reps done back to back before resting.
        Compound lift
        Uses several joints and muscles at once, like a squat or row.
        Isolation lift
        Targets one main muscle, like a curl or lateral raise.
        Hypertrophy
        The scientific term for muscle growth.
        DOMS
        Delayed onset muscle soreness. Normal soreness 1–2 days after training. Sharp or joint pain is not normal.

        FAQ

        Questions every beginner asks

        How long until I see results?

        Most beginners get stronger within the first few weeks, mostly because your body gets better at doing the lifts. Visible muscle usually takes a couple of months of consistent training and eating. Everyone's timeline is different, so compare yourself to last month, not to someone online.

        Should I be sore after every workout?

        No. Soreness is common when you start or try something new, then it usually fades. It isn't a sign of a good workout. Getting stronger over time is the sign that matters.

        Can I train if I'm still sore?

        Mild soreness is usually fine. A warm-up often makes it feel better. If you're so sore it changes how you move, or you feel sharp or joint pain, rest that area and see a doctor if it doesn't go away.

        Do I need supplements?

        No. Everything on this site works with regular food. Supplements aren't tightly regulated, so if you're thinking about one, talk to a doctor first, especially if you're under 18.

        Will cardio kill my gains?

        Not in normal amounts. Walking, biking, or a couple of short cardio sessions a week are good for your heart and recovery. Just make sure you're eating enough to cover the extra energy.

        Is lifting safe for teenagers?

        The American Academy of Pediatrics says kids and teens can benefit from strength training when it's properly supervised and focused on good technique. Start light, learn form first, and train with a coach or experienced adult.

        What if I miss a workout?

        Just do the next one. Missing one day doesn't undo anything. What matters is showing up most weeks for months.

        How do I know if it's working?

        Track three things: the weights in your log going up, your bodyweight trending slowly up if you're trying to gain, and a progress photo once a month in the same lighting.
